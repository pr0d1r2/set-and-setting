# Virtuous circle

Build beneficial feedback loops in which each useful result makes the next
useful action easier, faster, or more effective. Do not rely on repetition
alone: name the outcome, the behavior it enables, and the feedback path that
connects them.

## Creating a virtuous circle

- State the desired outcome and the next behavior it should reinforce. If the
  result does not increase the likelihood or effectiveness of that behavior,
  there is no reinforcing loop yet.
- Draw the shortest causal chain you can defend. Mark assumptions between each
  step and test the weakest link before investing in the whole cycle.
- Seed the loop with the smallest useful input. Prefer a reversible change that
  can produce evidence and momentum over a large launch that must succeed all
  at once.
- Shorten and clarify feedback. Make beneficial results visible soon enough for
  people or systems to connect them to the action that produced them.
- Reinvest gains into the next iteration. Turn saved time, learned knowledge,
  improved reliability, or user trust into an easier or better next action.
- Measure both the outcome and the loop. Track whether each iteration improves
  the desired result and whether that result actually strengthens the next
  iteration.
- Add limits and exit conditions. Positive feedback can amplify a proxy,
  concentrate risk, or become harmful after conditions change.

## Signals of violation

- A repeated activity is called a virtuous circle without evidence that its
  output improves the next iteration.
- The proposed loop depends on several untested causal assumptions and has no
  early probe for the weakest one.
- Feedback arrives too late or is too ambiguous to guide the next action.
- Gains are consumed as extra scope instead of reinvested in capability,
  quality, or lower effort.
- A proxy improves while the real outcome stalls or worsens.
- The loop is allowed to accelerate without a threshold, review point, or way
  to stop it.

## When reinforcement conflicts with control

A self-reinforcing loop is not inherently good. Its value depends on the
outcome it amplifies, and a virtuous circle can become a vicious one when a
proxy replaces the goal or side effects accumulate. Keep a balancing control:
inspect downstream effects, revisit the causal model, and stop or redesign the
loop when net results are no longer beneficial. Use [[consequences]] to examine
second-order effects, [[reality]] to update the model from observed results,
and [[progress]] to encode learning into the next iteration.

Based on the [virtuous cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicious_circle#Virtuous_cycles),
the beneficial counterpart to a vicious circle: a sequence whose results
reinforce the conditions that produce further beneficial results.
