# Hindsight bias

Judge past decisions from the information available when they were made,
not from the outcome now known. Hindsight bias makes an observed outcome
feel more predictable or inevitable than it was and can rewrite memory of
what was previously believed.

## Countering hindsight bias

- Preserve the contemporaneous record. Before acting, write down the known
  facts, assumptions, alternatives, probabilities, and reasons for the
  choice. Prefer dated issues, logs, forecasts, and decision records over
  later recollection.
- Reconstruct the decision point without outcome knowledge. Build the
  timeline only from evidence available before the result, then compare it
  with the full timeline.
- Consider the opposite. Name plausible outcomes that did not occur and
  explain how the evidence at the time could have supported each one.
- Separate decision quality from outcome quality. A sound process can have
  a bad result, and a weak process can get lucky. Evaluate the reasoning
  before using the outcome to update future practice.
- Replace claims such as "obvious" and "inevitable" with the specific signal
  that was observable, who could access it, and what action it justified at
  the time.
- Compare forecasts with results. Score recorded predictions and confidence
  instead of asking people what they remember expecting.
- Run blameless reviews. Ask what made the action reasonable in context and
  which system changes would improve the next decision; do not infer
  negligence merely because the failure is clear in retrospect.

## Signals of violation

- A review relies on "we knew" without a timestamped prediction or record.
- The actual outcome is described as the only realistic path while
  pre-outcome alternatives disappear from the account.
- A decision is judged solely by whether it succeeded rather than by the
  evidence, uncertainty, and tradeoffs present when it was made.
- Reviewers treat a signal as obvious after being told exactly where to look.
- Confidence in future prediction rises without comparing earlier forecasts
  with their observed accuracy.

## Limits

Do not use uncertainty to excuse ignored evidence or avoid accountability.
When the contemporaneous record shows that a known risk was dismissed,
address that failure directly. The aim is fair, evidence-based learning:
recover the real decision context, preserve genuine foresight, and improve
the system without granting the outcome information that no one yet had.
